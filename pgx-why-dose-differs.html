Why the Same Dose Works Differently

Same drug, same dose, very different results. Meet pharmacogenomics through three gene-drug stories every pharmacist should know, and learn who decides what to do with a genetic result.

🟢 Beginner ⏱️ ~35 min 💊 Pharmacogenomics 🌐 No install
Educational content, not medical advice. Pharmacogenomic results should only guide treatment when they come from a validated clinical test and are interpreted by a qualified clinician using the current guideline.

Before you start

  • No coding needed. It helps to know what a gene and a variant are; if not, see The biology a coder needs.
  • Pharmacists and clinicians: you know the drugs. This track adds the genetics and the data side.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to: explain what pharmacogenomics is; tell a pharmacokinetic gene from a pharmacodynamic one; describe how CYP2C19, CYP2D6 and DPYD change the response to clopidogrel, codeine and fluoropyrimidines; and explain what CPIC guidelines and FDA labels do, and do not, tell you.

Same drug, same dose, different patients

Two patients receive the same drug at the same dose. One responds well. The other gets no benefit, or a serious side effect. Age, kidney function, other medicines and adherence all play a part, but for some drugs a large part of the difference is written in the patient's genes. Pharmacogenomics (PGx) studies how inherited genetic variation changes the way people respond to drugs, and uses that knowledge to choose the right drug and dose.

You will also see the word pharmacogenetics. Strictly, it means the effect of a single gene, while pharmacogenomics takes the whole-genome view, but in practice (including in CPIC guidelines and FDA documents) the two words are used for the same thing.

Two kinds of genes: exposure and target

TypeWhat the gene changesExamples
Pharmacokinetic (PK)How much drug, or active drug, reaches the body: absorption, activation, breakdown, removalCYP2C19, CYP2D6, DPYD (all enzymes)
Pharmacodynamic (PD)How the target responds at a given amount of drugOPRM1 (the opioid receptor), COMT

Almost all of the pharmacogenomics used in routine care today involves PK genes. For PD genes such as OPRM1 and COMT, the current CPIC opioid guideline found the evidence too weak to make any recommendation. So this track focuses on enzymes.

Three stories every pharmacist should know

1. CYP2C19 and clopidogrel: the prodrug that needs switching on

Clopidogrel is a prodrug: it protects against clots only after the liver enzyme CYP2C19 converts it into its active form. People with reduced or absent CYP2C19 activity make less active drug, so their platelets are less inhibited. In 2010 the FDA added a boxed warning to the clopidogrel (Plavix) label about a diminished antiplatelet effect in people with two loss-of-function CYP2C19 alleles, and in 2016 extended it to all patients taking the drug.

The current CPIC guideline (2022 update) recommends that, for patients with acute coronary syndrome or a stent procedure (PCI), intermediate and poor metabolizers avoid clopidogrel and receive prasugrel or ticagrelor if there is no contraindication. Increasing the clopidogrel dose is not recommended as a fix. Normal, rapid and ultrarapid metabolizers take the standard dose.

2. CYP2D6 and codeine: too little and too much are both dangerous

Codeine relieves pain mainly because CYP2D6 converts part of it into morphine. A poor metabolizer makes very little morphine and gets little pain relief; CPIC found no evidence that a higher codeine dose helps. An ultrarapid metabolizer makes more morphine than expected, with a risk of serious toxicity at normal doses. CPIC (2021) recommends that both groups avoid codeine and use an alternative. Several regulators advise against codeine in children under 12, and the US label warns against breastfeeding while taking it, because morphine passes into breast milk.

3. DPYD and fluoropyrimidines: when the drug cannot be cleared

The cancer drugs 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and its oral prodrug capecitabine are broken down mainly by the enzyme DPD, encoded by the gene DPYD. People with low DPD activity cannot clear the drug normally, and standard doses can cause severe, sometimes fatal, toxicity. CPIC recommends lower starting doses for partial deficiency and avoiding these drugs in complete deficiency. In February 2026 the FDA updated the capecitabine and 5-FU labels with a boxed warning that advises DPYD testing before starting treatment (unless treatment is needed immediately), a shift from warning about DPD deficiency to testing first.

Why ancestry matters

The variants that switch enzymes off are not equally common everywhere. Take CYP2C19*2, the most common no-function CYP2C19 allele:

South Asian33%

Admixed American13%
European (non-Finnish)15%

The allele is roughly twice as common in East and South Asian populations as in Europeans, so the same test finds more affected patients in some populations than in others. Gene panels designed mostly from European data can also miss variants that matter elsewhere: for DPYD, the variant c.557A>G is relatively common in people of African ancestry but is not on the usual four-variant panel.

Who decides what to do with a result?

  • CPIC (Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium) writes peer-reviewed guidelines that answer one question: "Given this genetic result, what should I prescribe?" CPIC guidelines deliberately do not say who should be tested. They are voluntary and are updated over time.
  • ClinPGx is the current home of CPIC guidelines and the PharmGKB knowledge base (PharmGKB and CPIC were recently brought together under the ClinPGx name).
  • The FDA puts pharmacogenomic information in drug labels and keeps a Table of Pharmacogenetic Associations. Being listed does not by itself mean the FDA recommends testing before prescribing.

Guidelines change: always check the live version

This lesson describes the guidelines as of October 2026. A CPIC update for DPYD is expected in late 2026, for example. Before acting on any result, open the current guideline on ClinPGx.

Check your understanding

A patient who is a CYP2C19 poor metabolizer receives a stent and is started on clopidogrel. Why is this a problem?
Clopidogrel is a prodrug. With little CYP2C19 activity, less of it becomes active, so platelets are less inhibited. CPIC recommends an alternative such as prasugrel or ticagrelor for poor and intermediate metabolizers after ACS or PCI.
Why does CPIC recommend avoiding codeine in both CYP2D6 poor metabolizers and ultrarapid metabolizers?
Codeine works through its conversion to morphine by CYP2D6. Too little conversion means no benefit; too much means dangerous morphine levels. Raising the dose for poor metabolizers is not supported by evidence.
For 5-FU, why is low DPD activity dangerous, while for clopidogrel low CYP2C19 activity mainly reduces benefit?
The direction of the effect depends on what the enzyme does: an enzyme that clears the active drug (DPD) causes toxicity when weak, while an enzyme that activates a prodrug (CYP2C19) causes loss of effect when weak.
What question does a CPIC guideline answer?
CPIC guidelines start from an existing result and translate it into prescribing advice. They deliberately do not recommend who should be tested.
CYP2C19*2 is about twice as common in East Asian populations as in European ones. What does this mean in practice?
Frequency differences change how often testing finds an actionable result, not what a result means for the individual. Decisions are made on the patient's own genotype, not their ancestry.
