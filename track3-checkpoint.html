Track 3 Checkpoint: Can You Put RNA-seq Together?

Ten new questions you have not seen in the lessons. Each one mixes ideas from more than one lesson, the way real analyses do. Take it before the capstone to find out what you already own and what to review.

Track checkpoint ~20 min In your browser, no code

What this checks

  • You can plan an RNA-seq experiment that can answer its question (replicates, batches).
  • You know which numbers go into which tool, and why (counts, TPM, vst).
  • You can read DESeq2 results with judgment, not just thresholds.
  • You can spot circular or misleading conclusions in heatmaps, enrichment and bulk data.

Before you start

  • Finish the Track 3 lessons on the roadmap, or at least the big picture, experimental design, DESeq2 and enrichment lessons.
  • No notes on the first try. The point is to find out what you can recall, which is also one of the best ways to make it stick.

How this checkpoint works

Answer all ten questions. When you finish, the page shows your score. 7 of 10 counts as a pass; aim for 8 or more before starting the capstone. Every explanation links to the lesson to review.

This is a self-check, not an exam. The answers live in the page, so it only works if you answer honestly before reading the explanation. If you score below 7, review the linked lessons, wait a day or two, then reload and try again. Coming back after a break helps you remember more than re-reading straight away.

The ten questions

Scenario questions. Pick the best answer; some wrong answers are tempting on purpose.

A collaborator sends you a table of TPM values for 3 control and 3 treated samples and asks for a DESeq2 analysis. What do you ask them for first?
DESeq2 models raw counts with a negative binomial distribution and does its own normalization. TPM and FPKM throw away the count scale that its statistics depend on, and log values are not counts at all. Review: RNA-seq normalization.
Your budget allows either 2 replicates per group sequenced at 60 million reads each, or 5 replicates per group at 25 million reads each. For finding differentially expressed genes, which is usually the better choice?
Variation between biological samples is usually the main limit on power. Once depth is reasonable, adding replicates helps more than adding reads (Liu et al. 2014). Very deep sequencing still matters for some goals, such as rare transcripts or isoforms. Review: Experimental design for RNA-seq.
You can only prepare 3 libraries per day, and you have 3 control and 3 treated samples. Which plan is best?
Each day must contain both conditions, otherwise day and treatment are confounded and no model or correction tool can separate them. With both conditions in each batch, adding day to the design lets DESeq2 account for it. Normalization only corrects for differences in sequencing depth and library composition, not batch effects (Leek et al. 2010). Review: Correcting batch effects.
Before running DESeq2, a PCA of your vst-transformed counts shows treated and control samples separating clearly along PC1. A colleague says: "Great, so most genes will be differentially expressed." What is wrong with this?
PCA is a quality-control and overview tool. A clean split is reassuring but says nothing about how many individual genes pass a statistical test. Review: Sample QC and PCA.
Your factor levels are control (the reference) and treated. Gene A has log2FoldChange = -2 and padj = 0.001. What does this mean?
A log2 fold change of -2 means 2 to the power of -2, which is one quarter: expression in treated is about 4 times lower than in the reference group. The sign depends on which level is the reference, so always check it. Review: DESeq2 lesson.
On your volcano plot, gene B has log2FoldChange = 5, padj = 0.4 and a baseMean of 3 (very few reads). How should you treat it?
Fold changes from genes with few counts swing wildly by chance. That is why DESeq2 offers shrinkage, and why you look at baseMean and the shrunken fold change (lfcShrink), not just the raw fold change, before trusting a large change (Love et al. 2014). Review: Volcano and MA plots.
You test 20,000 genes and call every gene with a raw p-value below 0.05 significant, giving 1,500 genes. Even if the treatment did nothing at all, roughly how many genes would you expect to pass?
A raw p-value cutoff of 0.05 lets about 5% of true-null tests through. With 20,000 tests that is about 1,000 false hits. This is why DESeq2 reports padj, which controls the false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Review: Multiple testing.
You pick the 50 genes with the smallest padj, draw a heatmap, and show that clustering perfectly separates treated from control samples. You present this as independent validation. Why is that weak?
Using the same data to select genes and then to show they separate the groups is called double dipping (Kriegeskorte et al. 2009). The heatmap is a useful picture of the result, not extra evidence for it. Validation needs new data or an independent method. Review: Heatmaps and clustering.
Your 200 significant liver genes are enriched for "lipid metabolism". You used all 20,000 human protein-coding genes as the background, but only 11,000 genes were expressed enough to be tested. What is the problem?
Genes expressed in liver are already rich in liver functions. If the background includes thousands of genes that were never testable, almost any liver gene list will look enriched for liver biology. Review: Functional enrichment.
Bulk RNA-seq of a tumour shows gene X is 2 times higher after treatment. Which explanation can bulk data alone NOT rule out?
Bulk RNA-seq averages over every cell in the sample. A rise can come from each cell making more RNA or from a change in which cell types are present. Single-cell RNA-seq can help tell these apart. Review: Single-cell RNA-seq intro.
