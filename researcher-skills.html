Mini-Course: Thinking Like a Researcher

The skills that turn a tool user into a researcher: reading papers efficiently, judging whether data can answer a question, asking questions you can actually answer, and checking and reporting your results so others can trust them. It ends with a graded final assessment.

🎓 Mini-course ⏱️ ~2 hr 🌐 In your browser ✅ Graded assessment

What you'll be able to do

  • Read a research paper in three passes, and know when to stop.
  • Pull the experimental design out of a methods section and judge whether a public dataset can answer your question.
  • Turn a vague interest into a question you can actually answer, with an analysis plan written before you look at the results.
  • Check your own results with sanity checks, write a methods paragraph someone else could repeat, and ask for help in a way that gets answers.

Before you start

  • No coding needed for most of it. Module 4 has one small in-browser Python box.
  • It helps to have seen an RNA-seq analysis, for example Your first RNA-seq analysis. The worked examples use the same real study (Himes et al. 2014) as the Track 3 capstone.

Why this mini-course exists

Most courses teach you to run tools. Research asks for something else: deciding what to run, on which data, and whether to believe the answer. These habits are rarely taught directly, yet they are what supervisors and employers notice first. This mini-course teaches them as concrete steps you can practise.

Module 1 of 4

Read a paper in three passes

Beginners often read a paper once, from the first word to the last, and come away tired and unsure what it said. Experienced researchers read in passes, going deeper only when the paper earns it. Keshav's widely used "three-pass" method is a good starting point:

PassWhat you readTimeYou should be able to say
1. SurveyTitle, abstract, introduction, section headings, figures (just glance), conclusions, and the reference list5 to 10 minutesWhat kind of paper this is, what question it asks, and whether it is worth more of your time
2. UnderstandThe whole paper, with care for the figures and their legends; skip heavy maths and supplementary detailsUp to about an hourThe main claims and the evidence behind each one, in your own words
3. RebuildEvery detail, especially the methods, as if you had to redo the work yourselfSeveral hoursExactly what was done, which assumptions were made, and where the weak points are

After the first pass, Keshav suggests checking the five Cs: Category (what kind of paper: a new method, a new dataset, a biological finding, a review?), Context (which earlier work it builds on), Correctness (do the assumptions look valid?), Contributions (what is actually new?) and Clarity (is it well written?). Many papers stop there, and that is fine: you will read far more papers at pass 1 than at pass 3.

Figures first

In biology the evidence lives in the figures. A useful habit in pass 2: for each figure, write one sentence saying what it shows and one saying what claim it supports. If you cannot link a claim in the abstract to a figure, that claim deserves suspicion.

Try it (10 minutes): do a first pass on Himes et al. 2014 (free to read). Write down the five Cs. Then compare with ours below.
Our five Cs for Himes et al. 2014

Category: a biological finding, supported by a new RNA-seq dataset. Context: steroids treat asthma, but how they act in airway smooth muscle was poorly understood. Correctness: a clean paired design (each donor treated and untreated), but only 4 donors and cells grown in a dish. Contributions: a list of steroid-responsive genes, and CRISPLD2 as a new candidate followed up in the lab. Clarity: clear overall; one table header says "Ln" where the values are log2 (you meet this in the capstone).

Module 2 of 4

Read the methods and judge a dataset

Before you reuse anyone's data, or trust their conclusion, you need the experimental design: what was compared with what, how many times, and what else changed along the way. It is in the methods section and the database record, and you can extract it with the same checklist every time:

AskWhy it mattersHimes et al. 2014
What is being compared?Defines the question the data can answerDexamethasone (1 µM, 18 h) versus untreated
What is a biological replicate here?Sets the real sample sizeA donor: 4 donors
Are samples paired or independent?Changes the right analysisPaired: each donor gives one treated and one untreated sample
What else differs between groups?Hidden differences can fake a resultNothing reported besides the treatment
Organism, cell type, tissueLimits how far results generaliseHuman airway smooth-muscle cells, grown in culture
Sequencing and processingNeeded to reprocess or compare75 bp paired-end reads; TopHat, Cufflinks and Cuffdiff on hg19
Where is the raw data?Without it, you cannot check anythingGEO GSE52778

The red flag that sinks studies: confounding

A confounder is anything that changes together with the thing you are testing. Imagine two designs for the same 8-sample experiment, sequenced on two machine runs:

Run 1Run 2Verdict
Design A4 untreated4 treatedConfounded: any difference could be the run, not the drug
Design B2 untreated + 2 treated2 untreated + 2 treatedBalanced: the run effect can be measured and removed

No analysis can rescue Design A, because the run and the treatment are perfectly mixed up. Batch effects like this are common in high-throughput biology and have led to wrong published conclusions. When you read a methods section, always ask: was anything processed in groups that line up with the conditions? If the paper does not say, treat that as missing information, not good news.

Is this public dataset usable?

Before spending a week on a public dataset, check five things on its database page (GEO, ArrayExpress, SRA):

  1. Raw data is available (FASTQ files in SRA or ENA), not only a processed table you cannot check.
  2. Sample information is complete: condition, replicate, and ideally batch, sex and age for every sample.
  3. The samples match the paper: same number and same groups as the methods describe.
  4. There are real biological replicates, ideally at least 3 per group.
  5. It actually fits your question: the right organism, tissue, condition and comparison.

Datasets that pass these checks follow the spirit of the FAIR principles: findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable.

Module 3 of 4

Ask a question you can answer, and plan before you look

"I want to study asthma genes" is an interest, not a question. A research question you can actually answer names the system, the comparison, the measurement and the data:

Too vagueAnswerable
What genes are involved in asthma?In human airway smooth-muscle cells, which genes change expression after 18 hours of dexamethasone, compared with untreated cells from the same donor (GSE52778)?
Is gene X important in cancer?Is gene X expressed at a different level in tumour versus matched normal tissue in a public cohort, and is that difference larger than the variation between patients?
What does this dataset show?Do the treated samples separate from the untreated ones on a PCA plot, and if not, what explains the main variation instead?

Write the plan before you see the results

If you try many analyses and keep the one that "works", you will almost always find something, even in pure noise. The protection is simple: write a short analysis plan first, and record any change you make later, with the reason. Five lines are enough:

Question: the answerable version, in one sentence.

Data: accession, samples used, and any you will exclude (and why, decided now).

Comparison and method: for example DESeq2, design ~ donor + treatment, adjusted p < 0.05.

Positive control: a result you expect if everything works, for example known steroid-response genes such as FKBP5 and TSC22D3 go up.

What would change my mind: the result that would make you conclude "no effect" or "the data cannot answer this".

The positive control is the most underused line. If a known effect does not show up, something is wrong with the data or the pipeline, and any new "discovery" from the same run deserves no trust.

Module 4 of 4

Check your results, write them up, ask for help

Sanity checks before you believe anything

  • Positive controls: do the effects you expected appear, in the right direction?
  • Look at the raw data for your top hits: plot the counts for each sample. One extreme sample can create a "significant" gene on its own.
  • Check the labels: on a PCA plot, do samples group the way the sample sheet says? Swapped labels are a common, silent error.
  • Is the size plausible? Zero significant genes, or half the genome, both deserve a second look before any biology.
  • Can you rerun it? Run the whole analysis again from the raw files with one command or notebook. If you cannot, nobody else can either.

Write a methods paragraph someone could repeat

A good methods paragraph lets a stranger repeat your work without asking you anything. It names the data source with its accession, the reference genome build, every tool with its version, the design or model, the filtering rule, the multiple-testing correction and the threshold. Try the checker below: press Run to see what the weak example is missing, then rewrite it (or paste your own) and run it again.

The checker only looks for the presence of each piece, not whether it is correct. A human reader is still the real test.

Show a paragraph that scores 7 of 7

"Gene-level read counts for the eight samples of GEO GSE52778 (four donors, untreated and dexamethasone-treated), aligned to GRCh37, were taken from the Bioconductor airway package. Genes with at least 10 reads in at least 4 samples were kept (16,139 genes). Differential expression was tested with DESeq2 1.42.0 in R 4.3.3 using the design ~ cell + dex to account for the paired donors. Genes with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value below 0.05 were called significant."

Ask for help in a way that gets answers

Forums such as Biostars and the Bioconductor support site are full of people willing to help, but they answer clear questions first. A good question includes:

  1. What you are trying to do, in one sentence.
  2. The exact command or code you ran, as text, not a screenshot.
  3. The full error message, copied exactly.
  4. Versions of the tool, R or Python, and your operating system (in R, the output of sessionInfo()).
  5. What you already tried, and a tiny example that reproduces the problem if you can make one.

Making that tiny example often solves the problem before you post it, which is a sign you are learning to debug like a researcher.

✅ Final assessment

Five questions covering the whole mini-course. Aim for at least 4 out of 5 correct. These are reasoning questions, not recall, so treat it like the real thing.

You have 40 new papers on a topic and two days. What is the best use of the three-pass method?
A 5 to 10 minute first pass tells you which papers deserve more time. Most will stop at pass 1; the handful that matter get passes 2 and 3.
A study sequenced all 6 control samples in January and all 6 treated samples in March. It reports 2,000 treatment-responsive genes. What is the main problem?
When every control was processed at one time and every treated sample at another, the batch and the treatment cannot be separated. No statistical tool can untangle them afterwards.
Which research question is the most answerable with a public RNA-seq dataset?
It names the system (mouse liver), the comparison (high-fat diet versus control), the measurement (gene expression) and the data (an accession). The others are interests, not questions a dataset can settle.
Your analysis finds 300 significant genes, but FKBP5, a gene known to respond strongly to your treatment, shows no change. What should you do first?
A known effect that goes missing is a warning sign. Swapped labels, a wrong comparison direction or a processing error would explain it, and would also make the other 300 genes untrustworthy.
Which methods sentence is most useful to someone who wants to repeat your work?
Only the first names the data (accession), the tool with its version, the model and the threshold. "Standard methods" and "best practices" cannot be repeated by anyone.
