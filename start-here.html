From biology, health or pharmacy

This is the path the founder of BioNexus took, from pharmacy into bioinformatics. You are not starting from zero: you already think like a scientist.

You already have

The biology, a feel for experiments and controls, and often clinical or drug knowledge that many bioinformaticians lack.

What to build

Reading and writing simple code, handling data files, and the command line. It feels strange at first and gets easier quickly.

  1. What bioinformatics actually is · 20 min
    The big picture, and where your background fits.
  2. The computing a biologist needs · 30 min
    Variables, scripts, the terminal: the words explained in lab terms.
  3. Your first 10 lines of Python · 45 min
    Run real code in your browser on a DNA sequence.
  4. Python basics (Level 1) · a few hours, at your pace
    The core of coding, with practice exercises.
  5. Command-line basics (Level 1) · a few hours, at your pace
    How almost every bioinformatics tool is run.
  6. Sequences, FASTA and FASTQ · 45 min
    Your first real biological data files.
  7. The RNA-seq big picture · 30 min
    Then the flagship first RNA-seq analysis (2 to 3 hours).

You can skim for now: the biology a coder needs, since you know it. Pharmacists and clinicians: Track 7, pharmacogenomics, builds directly on what you know. And read the transition playbook early; it was written for you.

"Is it too late to learn to code?"

No. Many working bioinformaticians started in the lab or clinic. Your field knowledge is an advantage, because you can tell when a result does not make biological sense.

From computing, engineering or data

You can already write code, so you will move fast through the technical parts. The hard part is different: biology has its own vocabulary, file conventions and statistical traps that general programming does not teach.

You already have

Programming, the command line, version control and comfort with large data.

What to build

Enough biology to ask good questions, the field's file formats and their traps, and why experimental design limits what any analysis can show.

  1. What bioinformatics actually is · 20 min
    What the field does, beyond "code for biology".
  2. The biology a coder needs · 30 min
    DNA, RNA, proteins and genes, explained with programming analogies.
  3. The file-format alphabet soup · 40 min
    FASTQ, SAM/BAM, VCF, GFF and BED, and what each is for.
  4. Genomic coordinates: 0-based vs 1-based · 25 min
    The classic off-by-one trap between formats.
  5. How NGS works · 20 min
    Where the data comes from, and why it is messy.
  6. Experimental design for RNA-seq · 25 min
    Replicates and batches: no code can rescue a broken design.
  7. The multiple-testing problem · 45 min
    Testing 20,000 genes at once changes how you read p-values.

You can skip: the Foundations install and basics pages for languages you already use. If you only know Python, do R basics, because the standard RNA-seq tools (DESeq2 and Bioconductor) are written in R.

"Can I just learn the tools?"

You can run them quickly, but judging whether a result is right needs the biology and the design. That is where most analysis errors hide, and it is what employers look for.

Starting from scratch

No science degree and no coding? That is completely fine. The Introduction was written with you in mind, and nothing assumes knowledge you have not been given.

You already have

Curiosity, and a web browser. That is all the first lessons need.

What to build

A little biology and a little coding, side by side, in small steps.

  1. What bioinformatics actually is · 20 min
    What the field is, in plain words.
  2. The map: what you can do in 3 months · 15 min
    An honest picture of the road ahead.
  3. The biology you need · 30 min
    DNA, RNA and proteins, with no assumed knowledge.
  4. The computing you need · 30 min
    What code, scripts and the terminal actually are.
  5. Your first 10 lines of Python · 45 min
    Real code, in your browser, nothing to install.
  6. Introduction checkpoint · 20 min
    Ten friendly questions to check you are ready.
  7. Python basics (Level 1) · a few hours, at your pace
    Then the Foundations checkpoint and on to Track 1.

Go at your own pace. About 20 to 30 minutes a day is enough to make real progress. If anything goes wrong, the Help page covers the most common problems.

"What if I get stuck?"

Getting stuck is part of learning to code, for everyone. Every lesson explains its jargon, the Glossary has plain-English definitions, and the feedback button on every page reaches a real person.
