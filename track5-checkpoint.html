Track 5 Checkpoint: Can You Think Statistically?

Ten new questions you have not seen in the lessons. Each one is a decision a real analysis forces on you: which test, what a number means, and when a result is less solid than it looks. Take it at the end of Track 5 to find out what you already own and what to review.

Track checkpoint ~20 min In your browser, no code

What this checks

  • You can pick a test that matches the study design.
  • You can read confidence intervals, correlations and FDR correctly.
  • You can spot weak designs: small pilots, pseudo-replication, extrapolation.
  • You can recognise analysis choices that inflate false positives.

Before you start

  • Finish the Track 5 lessons on the roadmap.
  • No notes on the first try. The point is to find out what you can recall, which is also one of the best ways to make it stick.

How this checkpoint works

Answer all ten questions. When you finish, the page shows your score. 7 of 10 counts as a pass; aim for 8 or more before moving on. Every explanation links to the lesson to review.

This is a self-check, not an exam. The answers live in the page, so it only works if you answer honestly before reading the explanation. If you score below 7, review the linked lessons, wait a day or two, then reload and try again. Coming back after a break helps you remember more than re-reading straight away.

The ten questions

Scenario questions. Pick the best answer; some wrong answers are tempting on purpose.

You measure a blood marker in the same 8 patients before and after a drug. Which comparison fits this design?
Each patient is measured twice, so the measurements are linked. A paired test works on each person's own change and removes person-to-person differences, which usually gives much more power than treating the groups as independent. Review: Which statistical test should I use?.
Across 100 human tissues, genes A and B have a correlation of 0.9. A colleague concludes that gene A switches on gene B. What is the main problem?
A strong correlation tells you two things move together, not why. A third factor, such as tissue type, can drive both. The most direct test of cause is an experiment, such as knocking gene A down and measuring gene B. Review: Stats you can't avoid and Linear models.
A gene's log2 fold change has a 95% confidence interval from 0.1 to 2.5. What is the most useful way to read it?
The interval excludes 0, so the data point to an increase, but a range from 0.1 to 2.5 means the size is uncertain. Reporting the interval, not just a p-value, shows this honestly (Wasserstein and Lazar 2016). Strictly, 95% refers to how often such intervals capture the true value over repeated studies (Krzywinski and Altman 2013). Review: Stats you can't avoid (confidence intervals).
A pilot study with 3 samples per group found a huge effect. You plan the main study's sample size using that effect size. What is the risk?
With tiny samples, only results that happen to be large reach significance, so published or pilot effects are often inflated, sometimes called the winner's curse or a Type M error (Button et al. 2013; Gelman and Carlin 2014). Review: Power and sample size.
You compare treated and control samples, but half were processed in Lab 1 and half in Lab 2, with both conditions in each lab. Which analysis fits best?
A linear model can include known sources of variation alongside the effect you care about, so each effect is estimated with the others held fixed (Krzywinski and Altman 2015). This is the same idea behind DESeq2 designs such as ~ batch + condition. Review: Linear models.
A study measures 10 cells from each of 3 treated and 3 control mice, then runs a t-test with n = 30 per group. What is wrong?
This is called pseudo-replication. Cells from one animal share that animal's genetics and environment, so they are not independent replicates. Average per mouse, or use a model that accounts for mouse as a grouping factor (Lazic 2010). Review: Power and sample size (pseudo-replication).
You fit a straight line of response against drug dose for doses from 0 to 10 mg. The fit is excellent. Can you use it to predict the response at 100 mg?
Regression describes the data you have. Predicting far outside the measured range (extrapolation) assumes the same straight line continues, which biology rarely guarantees (see Altman and Krzywinski 2015 on what a regression line describes). Review: Linear models (do not predict beyond your data).
Two variables have a Pearson correlation of 0.8. When you finally plot them, the cloud is flat except for one extreme point far to the upper right. What should you conclude?
Summary numbers can hide very different data, as Anscombe showed with four datasets that share the same statistics (Anscombe 1973). Always plot first, check whether the correlation survives without that point, and be open about any point you treat specially. Review: Plots that tell the truth and Which statistical test.
Your DESeq2 analysis reports 400 genes with padj < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg). What does that 0.05 mean for this list?
Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false discovery rate: the expected share of false positives among the genes you call. It is an average over lists like this one, not an exact count and not a per-gene probability (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Review: Multiple testing.
An analyst tries six normalization methods and three filtering rules, then reports the combination that gives the most significant genes. Why is this a problem?
Each extra analysis is another chance for noise to look like a finding. Picking the "best" one afterwards makes p-values look stronger than they are, one form of p-hacking (Nuzzo 2014). Review: Multiple testing.
