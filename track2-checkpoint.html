Track 2 Checkpoint: Can You Trust Your Variants?

Ten new questions you have not seen in the lessons. Each one mixes ideas from more than one lesson, from raw reads to a final variant call. Take it at the end of Track 2 to find out what you already own and what to review.

Track checkpoint ~20 min In your browser, no code

What this checks

  • You know the order of an NGS pipeline and why each step comes where it does.
  • You can read QC and alignment signals (adapters, soft-clipping, MAPQ, duplicates) and act on them.
  • You can judge whether a variant call is believable, using depth, allele fraction and mapping quality.
  • You can avoid classic silent errors: genome builds, 0- vs 1-based coordinates, and one-size-fits-all filters.

Before you start

  • Finish the Track 2 lessons on the roadmap. Two questions also use the Track 1 lessons on genome builds and genomic coordinates.
  • No notes on the first try. The point is to find out what you can recall, which is also one of the best ways to make it stick.

How this checkpoint works

Answer all ten questions. When you finish, the page shows your score. 7 of 10 counts as a pass; aim for 8 or more before moving on. Every explanation links to the lesson to review.

This is a self-check, not an exam. The answers live in the page, so it only works if you answer honestly before reading the explanation. If you score below 7, review the linked lessons, wait a day or two, then reload and try again. Coming back after a break helps you remember more than re-reading straight away.

The ten questions

Scenario questions. Pick the best answer; some wrong answers are tempting on purpose.

Put these steps of a DNA variant-calling pipeline in the right order.
You clean the reads first, then place them on the reference. Sorting and indexing make the BAM fast to search, duplicates are flagged so they do not count twice, and only then do you call variants. Review: How NGS works and samtools survival kit (marking duplicates).
FastQC on your 2×150 bp reads shows adapter content rising toward the 3' end of the reads. What most likely happened, and what do you do?
When the DNA insert is shorter than the read length, the end of the read runs into the adapter. That foreign sequence does not match the genome, so it should be trimmed, for example with fastp (Chen et al. 2018). Review: Trimming and filtering reads.
You aligned RNA-seq reads with BWA-MEM (a DNA aligner). Many reads near exon boundaries are soft-clipped or map poorly. Why?
A read from spliced mRNA can join two exons that sit far apart in the genome. A DNA aligner cannot jump over the intron, so it clips the read. Splice-aware aligners such as STAR handle this (Dobin et al. 2013). Review: Read alignment.
A variant is supported by 30 reads, but every one of them has MAPQ 0, and the region is a known repeat. How much do you trust it?
MAPQ measures how sure the aligner is about where a read came from. In repeats, reads from similar copies can pile up in the wrong place and create fake variants. Many callers ignore or down-weight low-MAPQ reads for this reason. Review: Reading a BAM file.
In a germline (inherited) sample from a diploid human, which allele fraction best fits a real heterozygous variant?
A diploid cell has two copies of each chromosome, so a heterozygous variant should appear in roughly half the reads, with some random spread. A few percent is more typical of sequencing errors, contamination, or (in tumours) a subclone. 100% fits a homozygous variant. Review: Variant calling.
Your sample shows 60% duplicate reads after marking duplicates. The library was made from very little DNA. What is the best reading of this?
PCR copies of one DNA molecule are not independent evidence. If they are not marked, one early PCR error copied many times can look like a well-supported variant. Library complexity means how many different DNA molecules your library contains. With very little input DNA there are fewer molecules, so PCR copies the same ones again and again. Review: samtools survival kit.
Your reads were aligned to GRCh38, but you look up your variant positions in a database that uses GRCh37 (hg19). Many positions do not match. Why?
Each genome build is a different version of the reference, and the same gene can sit at a different position in each. Always record the build, and only compare positions within one build. Review: Genome builds.
A BED file contains the line chr1  999  1000. Which base does it describe, in the 1-based numbering used by VCF and by samtools regions?
BED is 0-based and half-open: start 999, end 1000 covers one base, which is base 1000 when you count from 1. This off-by-one difference is a classic silent bug when moving between BED and VCF. Review: Genomic coordinates.
Your targeted panel reports a mean depth of 30x. Can you conclude every target region is well covered?
Coverage is rarely even. Breadth of coverage, the fraction of target bases reaching a minimum depth, tells you where you can and cannot call variants. samtools depth -a reports every position, including 0x ones, and samtools coverage summarises a chromosome or region (Danecek et al. 2021). Review: Reading a BAM file and samtools survival kit.
Your whole genome was sequenced at about 15x. You filter calls with QUAL<50 || DP<30. Almost no variants survive. What went wrong?
Thresholds are not universal. At 15x most sites have a depth near 15, so requiring 30 removes almost everything. Look at the depth distribution first, then choose cut-offs that remove the weak low-depth tail (and, often, unusually high-depth sites that can signal repeats). Review: Variant calling.
