Star Alleles, Diplotypes and Phenotypes

How does a list of DNA variants become the words "poor metabolizer" on a report? Learn the translation chain, calculate CYP2D6 activity scores yourself, and see why some genes are much harder than others.

Educational content, not medical advice. Allele activity values and phenotype rules are updated over time; always use the current CPIC tables on ClinPGx for real results.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to: explain what a star allele and a diplotype are; read a CYP2C19 diplotype as a phenotype; calculate a CYP2D6 activity score and turn it into a phenotype; and explain why CYP2D6 and DPYD are harder to handle than CYP2C19.

From DNA letters to a word a prescriber can use

A lab result for a pharmacogene goes through three translations: variants (DNA changes) are grouped into alleles, the pair of alleles a person carries is a diplotype, and the diplotype is translated into a phenotype such as "poor metabolizer". Prescribing guidance is attached to the phenotype.

Star alleles

A star allele (written with an asterisk, such as *2) is a named haplotype: the combination of variants found together on one copy of a gene. Some star alleles are defined by a single variant, others by several. The official definitions are kept by PharmVar.

*1 is the reference allele: it means none of the tested variants were found. That makes *1 partly a default. If a lab tests only a few variants, a rare no-function variant it did not test will still be reported as *1.

Each allele is given a function: increased, normal, decreased, no function, or uncertain/unknown. For CYP2C19, the three alleles you will meet most are:

AlleleKey variantWhat it doesFunction
*2rs4244285 (c.681G>A)Disrupts splicing, so a faulty protein is madeNo function
*3rs4986893 (c.636G>A)Creates an early stop codonNo function
*17rs12248560 (c.-806C>T)In the promoter; increases how much enzyme is madeIncreased function

"Synonymous" is not the same as "harmless"

Variant annotation tools label *2's key variant as synonymous (it does not change the amino acid at its own position), yet it is a no-function allele because it breaks splicing. A pipeline that throws away synonymous variants would throw away the most important CYP2C19 variant.

From diplotype to phenotype: CYP2C19

The phenotype comes from the function of the two alleles together. These are the CPIC (2022) rules for the common CYP2C19 diplotypes:

DiplotypeAllelesPhenotype
*17/*17increased + increasedUltrarapid metabolizer
*1/*17normal + increasedRapid metabolizer
*1/*1normal + normalNormal metabolizer
*1/*2normal + no functionIntermediate metabolizer
*2/*17no function + increasedIntermediate metabolizer (the *17 does not make up for the lost copy)
*2/*2, *2/*3no function + no functionPoor metabolizer

"Normal metabolizer" replaced the older term "extensive metabolizer", which you may still see in older papers and labels. These standard terms come from a CPIC expert consensus (2017) so that results mean the same thing in every lab and health record.

Activity scores: CYP2D6

CYP2D6 has well over 100 named alleles, so instead of listing every pair, CPIC gives each allele an activity value and adds the two values to get an activity score:

Notice two things. CYP2D6 has no "rapid metabolizer" tier: the expert panel rejected one for lack of evidence, so the five-step ladder you saw for CYP2C19 is not universal. Why CYP2D6 is hard for bioinformatics

A CYP2D6 result from sequencing data is not a simple variant lookup. The gene can be deleted (*5), duplicated (xN), or mixed with its neighbour CYP2D7, a very similar non-working gene, to form hybrid genes. Short sequencing reads often cannot tell the two genes apart, so specialised tools such as Stargazer, Aldy and StellarPGx estimate how many copies of each gene are present from read depth. In one evaluation, 28% of samples carried CYP2D6 structural variation. Clinical labs also often report "duplication present" without saying which allele is duplicated.

DPYD: the gene without star alleles

DPYD is huge (about 950,000 bases) and most of its important variants are rare, so CPIC does not use star alleles for it. Variants are named by their HGVS name or rsID (old names such as *2A survive only as nicknames). Each variant gets an activity value, and the activity score is the sum of the two lowest values:

VariantrsIDActivity value
c.1905+1G>A (old name *2A)rs39182900 (no function)
c.1679T>G (old name *13)rs558860620 (no function)
c.2846A>Trs673767980.5 (decreased)
c.1129-5923C>G (HapB3)rs750171820.5 (decreased)*

* CPIC has announced that its pending DPYD update (expected late 2026) will change HapB3 to 0.75. Until it is published, the guideline value is 0.5.

So a person carrying one copy of c.2846A>T has an activity score of 1 + 0.5 = 1.5, and one copy of c.1905+1G>A gives 1 + 0 = 1. You will use these rules in the next lesson.

Check your understanding

A lab tests only three CYP2C19 variants and reports *1/*1. What does *1 actually tell you?
*1 is the reference allele, assigned when none of the tested variants are found. It is only as complete as the test.
What is the CPIC phenotype for CYP2C19 *2/*17?
One no-function allele plus one increased-function allele is classed as intermediate: the increased-function copy is not considered to make up for the lost one.
A patient is CYP2D6 *1/*41. What is the activity score and phenotype?
*1 = 1 and *41 = 0.5, so the score is 1.5, which falls in the normal range (1.25 to 2.25).
Why might a variant-filtering pipeline wrongly discard CYP2C19*2?
rs4244285 does not change its own amino acid, so annotators call it synonymous. It creates a faulty splice site, which is why *2 has no function.
Why is CYP2D6 harder to call from short-read sequencing than CYP2C19?
Structural variation and the near-identical CYP2D7 gene make CYP2D6 a copy-number and structural-variant problem, which is why dedicated callers exist.
