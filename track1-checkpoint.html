Track 1 Checkpoint: Can You Handle Real Biological Data?

Ten new questions you have not seen in the lessons. Each one is a situation you will meet with real files: quality scores, gene IDs, coordinates, strands, downloads and databases. Take it at the end of Track 1 to find out what you already own and what to review.

Track checkpoint ~20 min In your browser, no code

What this checks

  • You can read quality scores and coordinates correctly.
  • You can join and filter gene tables without silent errors.
  • You can pull sequences and data from public databases the right way.
  • You know which tool or file answers which question.

Before you start

  • Finish the Track 1 lessons on the roadmap.
  • No notes on the first try. The point is to find out what you can recall, which is also one of the best ways to make it stick.

How this checkpoint works

Answer all ten questions. When you finish, the page shows your score. 7 of 10 counts as a pass; aim for 8 or more before moving on. Every explanation links to the lesson to review.

This is a self-check, not an exam. The answers live in the page, so it only works if you answer honestly before reading the explanation. If you score below 7, review the linked lessons, wait a day or two, then reload and try again. Coming back after a break helps you remember more than re-reading straight away.

The ten questions

Scenario questions. Pick the best answer; some wrong answers are tempting on purpose.

A base in your FASTQ has a Phred quality score of 30. What is the chance that this base call is wrong?
Phred scores are logarithmic: Q = -10 × log10(error probability). Q10 means 1 in 10, Q20 means 1 in 100, and Q30 means 1 in 1,000 (Ewing and Green 1998). Each 10 points is ten times more accurate. Review: Sequences, FASTA and FASTQ.
A colleague opened your gene list in Excel and saved it. Now the genes SEPT2 and MARCH1 appear as "2-Sep" and "1-Mar". What is the lesson?
Spreadsheet auto-conversion of gene names into dates was found in about a fifth of papers with Excel gene lists in supplementary files (Ziemann et al. 2016). Reading and writing tables in code avoids it, and in 2020 HGNC renamed these genes (for example SEPT2 became SEPTIN2 and MARCH1 became MARCHF1) partly for this reason. Review: Pandas for biologists.
You join two gene tables on Ensembl IDs. One table has ENSG00000141510.17 and the other has ENSG00000141510. The join finds almost no matches. What is the fix?
The number after the dot is the version of that gene record. The part before the dot is the stable ID. Two tables made from different Ensembl releases often differ only in the version, so strip it before joining. A new version can mean the gene model was updated, but for gene-level joins, matching on the stable ID is standard. Symbols are worse, because they change and have aliases. Review: Biological databases and APIs.
A BED file has the interval chr2  1000  1500. A GTF has an exon at chr2, start 1001, end 1500. Are they the same stretch of DNA?
BED counts from 0 and does not include the end; GTF counts from 1 and includes both ends. Both describe 500 bases here. Add 1 to a BED start to get the 1-based start; the end stays the same. Review: Genomic coordinates.
Your pandas DataFrame genes has columns chrom and length. You want, for each chromosome, how many genes are longer than 10,000 bases. Which line does this?
Filter first, then group and count. The first option gives average length, the second compares a grouped object (which fails), and the last just takes the 10,000 shortest genes. Chromosomes with no gene over 10,000 bases will not appear in the result. Review: Pandas for biologists.
A gene is on the minus (-) strand according to the GTF. You cut its region out of the reference FASTA (which is written as the + strand). How do you get the sequence of the gene's own strand, read 5' to 3' (the same direction as its RNA)?
A reference FASTA stores only the + strand. A minus-strand gene is read from the other strand, in the opposite direction, so you need both steps: reverse and complement. In Biopython that is seq.reverse_complement() (Cock et al. 2009). Review: Biopython basics and File formats.
Your script requests 5,000 records from NCBI one at a time in a fast loop, with no email or API key set. After a while NCBI starts refusing your requests. What should you change?
NCBI's E-utilities allow 3 requests per second without an API key and 10 per second with one, and ask you to identify yourself (NCBI E-utilities guide). Batching many IDs into one request is faster and polite. Biopython's Entrez module asks for Entrez.email for this reason. Review: Where the data lives and Databases and APIs.
You have ChIP-seq peaks in peaks.bed and genes in genes.bed. You want every gene that has a peak within 5,000 bases of it. Which approach fits?
bedtools window finds overlaps after extending each interval by a set distance; -u reports each gene once. merge combines overlapping intervals, and complement finds regions not covered, so neither answers this question (Quinlan and Hall 2010). Review: bedtools: genome arithmetic.
A dataset page lists an MD5 checksum next to a 15 GB FASTQ file. After downloading, why compare it to the MD5 of your copy?
A checksum is a fingerprint of the exact bytes. If even one byte differs, the MD5 will almost certainly be different. Large downloads sometimes stop early without an obvious error, and a truncated FASTQ can silently lose reads. Review: Where the data lives.
A paper says its data is "available on GEO". You want the raw reads to re-run the whole analysis yourself. Where do you look?
GEO describes the experiment and often stores processed results, such as count tables. The raw sequencing reads are stored in SRA and linked from GEO (Barrett et al. 2013). Controlled-access human data are held in dbGaP or EGA instead. Review: Where the data lives.
